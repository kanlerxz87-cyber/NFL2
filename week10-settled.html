Thirteen settled games × four predictors. A spread column holds the predicted HOME margin. Error is absolute, in points, against the official final score. The do-nothing predictor writes the weeks 1-9 league average every time, and it is the bar the others have to clear. The first game (WAS 24 at NYG 21) is the one the narrative below was written around.

Read the table in this order:

  1. Our spread lost to knowing nothing. We published NYG −2.5. The Giants lost by 3. A predictor that wrote 0 every week was off by 3.0; we were off by 5.5. The model's lean cost 2.5 points against a baseline with no team information in it.
  2. Our total beat the archive by 4.0 points, and that is not the win it looks like. We published 45.5 against an actual 45. The archived community price published 40.5. But the league mean total is 45.93 — so the archive was the outlier, not us, and our 45.5 was within half a point of the number anyone could have written down from the standings page.
  3. The simplest model on the site was also the worst on this game. The points-only fair line (8.0 spread error, 3.0 total error) is dominated by a season average.
  4. Nobody covered the spread. Every one of the four predictors had the Giants winning; the Giants lost by 3.

    The thirteen-game aggregate — the first honest multi-game look

    The same computed register now carries an aggregate block over all thirteen settled games, recomputed on every build. Read it against the same arithmetic:

    • Spread: our origination MAE 10.35 points vs 13.54 for knowing nothing — 3.19 points better over 13 games, and 0.46 better than the archived community price (10.81, n=13).
    • Total: our origination MAE 9.85 points vs 8.61 for knowing nothing — 1.24 points worse, and 1.27 worse than the archive (8.58, n=13).
    • n=13 of the ~1,382 games the spread comparison requires. One good week is exactly as uninformative as one bad week; the leaderboard stays empty and nothing here is a claim of skill.

      • The one finding worth acting on

      Our 0.5-point total error looks like the best number on the site. It is the number most likely to be luck, and the register above says why in two lines:

      • This game was unusually average. Its total landed 0.93 points from the weeks 1-9 league mean. Only 8 of the 136 verified games (5.9%) are that close to the mean or closer.
      • A naive total predictor lands within 0.5 points of the actual total in 3.7% of games (5 of 136).

        • So the game handed every total predictor a near-free shot, and we took it. The right conclusion is not "our total model works" — it is that a single game carries no information about whether it works, because the error we are measuring is dominated by how average the game turned out to be, not by what the model knew.

        How much evidence it would actually take

        The 136-game log gives a standard deviation of 14.31 points on game totals and 13.27 on home margin. Detecting a 1.0-point difference in mean absolute error at 80% power and a two-sided 5% level needs:

        QuantityGames requiredWhat that is
        Totals1,607about ten NFL seasons
        Spreads1,382about nine NFL seasons

        One settled game is 0.07% of that; the thirteen settled so far are 0.94% of it. Gate G-08 on this site sets n ≥ 400 for any strategy claim; this page is the arithmetic underneath it, measured rather than asserted.

        What this page does not do

        • It does not add WAS–NYG to the backtest corpus. That corpus is deliberately frozen — see the note below.
        • It does not grade a strategy. No strategy on the leaderboard has a settled leg, because none was registered before a corpus existed (S-26).
        • It does not use the injury or depth data as a feature. The availability columns on the Week 10 origination table are counts, not coefficients, because no availability effect has been measured yet (Q-36).

          • Why the result lives in its own file

          data/games_2026_wk10_played.csv is append-only and separate from data/games_2026_wk1_9.csv.

          Suggestion S-26 asks for the weeks 1–9 file to be registered as the pre-registered backtest corpus, with its SHA-256 published so that a later edit invalidates every grade built on it. Appending week 10 to that file would silently re-baseline in-sample work that was already published against it. So the corpus is left byte-identical and its hash is recorded in the re-verification register at the bottom of this page instead:
