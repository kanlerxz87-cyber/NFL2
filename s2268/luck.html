Removing luck

Exact tests, a false-discovery correction, a seeded Monte Carlo, and the sample size each result would need.

On this pageThe headline numberThe four instrumentsEvery candidate testedThe rest signal
“Remove luck from the equation.” You cannot remove it, but you can measure it, and you can refuse to promote anything that has not been separated from it. Four instruments do that here: an exact binomial test, a false-discovery correction across the whole candidate set, a seeded Monte Carlo that asks what ten coin-flipping selectors would have produced, and the sample size each observed effect would need before it could be believed.

The headline number

Ten candidates were scored. The best hit rate achieved is 0.7917. Ten selectors flipping coins, given the same number of decisions each, produce a best-of-ten of 0.6101 on average and 0.7083 or better one time in twenty. So the observed best does clear the luck threshold — and it does so on 24 decisions, which is below this build's own 40-decision floor and far below the 21 the effect size would nominally need.

The trap on this page, stated plainly

P-REST wins 19 of 24 — 79.2%, the best number on the site, with a p value of 0.0066 that survives the false-discovery correction. It is also the smallest sample on the page, it is one season, it is six weeks, and the effect is larger than any rest effect has a right to be. A large effect on a small sample is the classic signature of something that will not repeat. It is published because it is what the data says, and it is not promoted because our own 40-decision floor exists precisely to stop a number like this from being promoted.
