Spreads and totals, scored against the only baselines that matter: the ones that know nothing.

On this pageThe headlineEvery predictor scoredWhy the model failedFrozen coefficients
Three ways to predict the home margin and three to predict the game total, scored on the same 72 holdout games. Two of the six are do-nothing baselines, and they are the ones that matter: a model that cannot beat them has no value, however sophisticated it looks.

The headline

Our originated spread misses by 11.03 points per game. Predicting that the home team wins by nothing at all misses by 10.92. Our originated total misses by 11.21 against 11.18 for the point-in-time league mean.

In other words: on this holdout, our own lines do not beat knowing nothing. That is the finding, it is the same wall session 63ac21e9 hit with thirteen seasons of data (spread MAE 10.25 against the market's 9.94), and publishing it is worth more than publishing a tuned number that would not survive next week.

Why the model failed, and what that means

The slope fitted on weeks 2–4 for the spread is small enough that, after shrinkage, the model barely moves off the home-field baseline; the correlation between its prediction and the actual margin is 0.031 with a p value of 0.80. The total model's slope is negative, which is to say the training window told it that teams with high scoring rates would play in lower-scoring games. On 48 games that is noise, and the shrinkage only partly damps it.

The honest reading is that nine weeks of one season is not enough to fit anything. The league's own scoring environment moves, opponent quality is not adjusted for, and the only feature with real signal in this data — the point-differential gap — correlates with the actual margin at r = 0.26 (p = 0.026), which explains about 7% of the variance and still does not translate into a better point forecast than zero.
