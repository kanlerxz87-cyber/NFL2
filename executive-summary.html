The one-paragraph version

The collection layer now exists and is genuinely verified: 254 rows of the official Week 10 practice report across 24 of the 28 clubs playing that week, 227 official roster and depth events, 32 reconciled standings rows and a 14-game schedule, every row carrying a link to the official page it came from and nothing inferred to fill a gap. The analysis layer is specified in full — line-origination architecture, 18 signal families, 14 validation gates, a correlation framework with twelve pair classes, a sample-size protocol for removing luck, and a 14-entry strategy registry — but it produces no numbers yet, because two inputs do not exist in this project: licensed market prices and player-level participation data. The honest summary is that the pipeline and the rules are real, the data spine is real, and every performance claim you might want from this system is currently un-earnable rather than absent.

Five findings that matter for decisions

  1. The injury hub is a practice document, not an availability answer. On the day we fetched, only 6 of 254 rows carried a game status, and the Injury column was populated for 6 — the same six filings. Anyone modelling availability off this table without the prose feed is modelling an empty column. (FL-02, FL-08)
  2. "No Injuries Reported" is a data state, not a health statement — and so is a missing club. The live Week 10 hub labels only two clubs that way (LAC, BAL). Two more of the 28 playing clubs are simply absent from our file (SEA, CIN), which is a collection gap and not a claim those clubs are healthy. A third (IND) is partial. Reading any of the three states as zero is the most likely silent error in this system, so the snapshot now reports coverage as 24 of 28 and refuses to phrase it as a sentence. (FL-01, FL-24)
  3. There is no official depth chart to buy or scrape. The obvious URL is a 400-style error page, so the requirement about second, third and fourth-stringers has to be met by reconstruction — roster status, participation, designations, then actual playing time. That is a build item, not a lookup. (FL-06)
  4. Roster churn in 2026 is large enough to break joins. Players appear on clubs a naive pipeline would flag as errors; four of the surprising cases are independently confirmed by the same official page. We transcribed everything as published and made the 32-team roster sweep a gate (G-02) rather than quietly "fixing" the league's own data. (FL-03)
  5. The parlay and prop-combo problem is a correlation problem before it is a pricing problem. Same-game legs share a cause; independent-leg multiplication overstates parlay value; and the one piece of public research quantifying a cross-game parlay markup has an openly disputed matching method. We therefore publish the framework and the signs we can defend, leave every rho value blank, and publish the two correlations that can be measured from real game scores — C-07 at r = +0.076 and C-08 at r = −0.161 — both with intervals that include zero.     2. Approve the pre-registration rule that backtest numbers never appear on the leaderboard (S-04) — this is the decision that keeps the competition honest.

      5. What the 12 November re-verification changed

      This session did not take the previous build's word for anything. It re-fetched the official standings, the Week 10 fixture list and all seven rendered chunks of the injury hub, and compared them row by row against data/. The result is worth stating plainly because it cuts both ways.

      What held up exactly. All 32 of 32 standings rows matched the live NFL page on wins, losses, ties, winning percentage, points for, points against, net points and streak. All 14 of 14 Week 10 fixtures matched on date, kickoff time, broadcaster and venue. Every one of the 254 injury rows that is in the file matched the league's own table on name, position and practice status — there is no fabricated row anywhere.

      What did not hold up. The published prose claimed more than the data supported, in four places:

      ClaimRealityNow
      The snapshot covers 28 clubsit holds 24 of the 28 playingreported as 24 of 28, derived from the fixture list
      Empty blocks are SEA, CIN, LAC, BALonly LAC and BAL say "No Injuries Reported"; SEA and CIN publish full blocks we never ingestedFL-24 published, the stale assertion in FL-01 corrected
      25 suggestions / 28 questions / 15 flags29 / 29 / 22 registers actually holdall corrected, and now guarded by an audit

      The mechanism matters more than the errors. tools/build_site.py had hard-coded the empty-club list and the 28 clubs subtitle, so both were immune to the truth of the file next to them. Eight passing audit suites never caught it, because every one of them compares the site to data/ and none compares data/ to the league. That gap is now closed for these cases: the club set is derived from the fixture list, and two new checks fail the build rather than warn. Both were tested with negative controls, which is recorded on the verification method page.

      The lesson, for whoever runs next. A number a file can be asked about must either be measured from that file on every build or be absent. Prose counts rot silently, and a green audit suite is not evidence that the data is still true — only that the site still agrees with it.

      What the 13 November settlement changed

      One Week 10 game became official after the previous build — Washington 24, NY Giants 21 on Thursday 12 November — and it is the first result any line on this site has been measured against. It is worth reading carefully, because it is a small sample that produces one flattering number and one unflattering one.

      What we publishedWhat happenedRead
      Our spread, NYG −2.5Giants lost by 35.50 pts of error. A predictor that wrote the league average of 0.00 every week was off by 3.00. Our lean cost 2.5 points against knowing nothing.
      Our total, 45.5Actual total 450.50 pts of error — the best number on the site, and the least meaningful.

      The reason the 0.5 does not mean what it looks like is on the Settled Week 10 page: this game's total landed 0.93 points from the league mean, and only 8 of the 136 verified games are that average or closer. A near-average game hands every predictor a cheap win. Detecting a 1.0-point difference in mean absolute error at 80% power needs roughly 1,400–1,600 games; one settled game is 0.07% of that.

      Two consequences for the programme:

      1. The standings refresh exposed a process gap, not a data one. NYG and WAS were each one game stale. The two rows are corrected, but no cumulative table on this site states when it was read, so the next staleness will be just as invisible. That is FL-35, Q-40 and S-43.
      2. The backtest corpus is now explicitly frozen. The weeks 1–9 file keeps its published SHA-256 (f0dffce0…6f33) and week 10 results go into a separate append-only file, so no grade already published is silently re-baselined by a refresh.

        3. The settlement pipeline works end to end. That is genuinely new and genuinely useful — it is the mechanism the leaderboard needs. It is not evidence that the model works, and nothing yet published could be.

        Standing risk to name plainly

        Everything on the blue pages is a specification written before the data existed. Specifications are cheap in this domain and the record of betting models is poor; the gates and the negative controls (ST-12 do-nothing, ST-13 public-news echo) exist precisely so that a specification has to survive contact with results. Until then the correct posture toward any number on the origination pages is scepticism, and the site is built so that the blanks are visible rather than hidden.

        Two defects found while doing it are worth reading before any next step, because both are the kind that survive a passing test:

        • A cross-source join silently dropped a whole club. The community archive writes ARI and LA where the league writes AZ and LAR; without an alias table the join loses all nine Arizona games and the model still reports an error rate (FL-28, S-36).
        • The register asserted something the repository contradicted. "No price data of any kind" sat next to 20,490 rows of vendored prices, and the file named closing_lines.csv stops in 2018 while the only 2026 prices live in games.csv. The upstream schema that is supposed to define those columns was last edited 13 May 2020 and never says whether the price is an open or a close (FL-29, FL-30, Price archive).

          • The requirement that is still not met, stated plainly: 2nd/3rd/4th-string deficiency tracking works — for two clubs. The join pattern is proven end to end on Seattle and Los Angeles (a first-string quarterback out with a limited second-stringer and an unreserved third-stringer behind him; a second-string nose tackle out and a third in; a long snapper with no backup listed on the club's own chart), and it produces the deficiency signal the standings cannot see. Scaling it is a transcription cadence decision (Q-37, S-39), not a research problem.

          What to decide now, in order: (1) start the daily stored injury snapshot so availability signals have a history — it is worth more every day it runs and it unblocks the largest part of the brief (S-35, Q-36); (2) adopt the alias table plus a join-row-count check (S-36); (3) admit the paired-error rule to the gates so a strategy cannot promote itself on an easy slate (S-38); (4) decide the price-vintage question, because it changes the wording of every CLV claim in the project (Q-35).
